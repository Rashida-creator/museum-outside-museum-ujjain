NO. 01 — FOOD LANE
Artefact: an iron tawa, blackened and smooth from years of batter, oil, and flame. Original illustration.

The Tawa That Never Retires

Old-city food lane · a generic working stall

1 — THE ARTEFACT

An iron tawa seasoned over years of daily use. Its scratches are its service record; it is never scrubbed raw.

2 — THE STORY

Street stalls across old Ujjain cook on iron tawas kept in use for years. Cooks treat the seasoned surface itself as part of the flavour.

3 — THE INTERPRETATION

The tool has outlasted menus, prices, perhaps the stall itself. It remembers every hand that held its handle.

4 — THE QUESTION

When does a tool become part of someone’s identity?

Sources: everyday market observation (generic setting); District Gazetteer, Ujjain — food trade (general). Interpretive exhibit; no real business depicted. Medium: original vector illustration.
NO. 02 — MORNING KITCHEN
Artefact: a cook’s hands at work — mixing, shaping, sprinkling, judging by touch. Face never shown.

The Hands That Know

Morning kitchen · touch and repetition

1 — THE ARTEFACT

Hands mixing, shaping, sprinkling, judging texture by touch alone. The knowledge lives in the fingers.

2 — THE STORY

In old-city kitchens, measures live in the body: a pinch, a handful, dough judged by feel. Recipes travel by watching, not by writing.

3 — THE INTERPRETATION

What was never written down can never be looked up. This knowledge exists only while hands keep repeating it.

4 — THE QUESTION

Can knowledge disappear even when a recipe survives?

Sources: everyday kitchen observation (generic setting); Gazetteer — food culture (general). Interpretive exhibit. Medium: original vector illustration.
NO. 03 — PROVISIONS LANE
Artefact: a mechanical scale — brass pans, trembling needle, weights worn smooth by handling.

The Scale of Trust

Provisions lane · weighed in public

1 — THE ARTEFACT

A mechanical weighing scale. Goods added grain by grain until the needle rests — the customer watching throughout.

2 — THE STORY

Traditional provisions shops still weigh on mechanical scales, openly, at the counter. Every movement of the pan is visible to both sides.

3 — THE INTERPRETATION

The scale measures weight in public so that trust needs no measuring. Transparency is the mechanism.

4 — THE QUESTION

Can trust be measured?

Sources: everyday market observation (generic setting); Gazetteer — bazaar commerce (general). Interpretive exhibit. Medium: original vector illustration.
NO. 04 — THE COUNTER
Artefact: a parcel in brown paper — corners folded sharp, string knotted, handed over with a tap.

The Art of Wrapping

Shop counter · paper and string

1 — THE ARTEFACT

A purchase wrapped in brown paper: each corner folded in seconds, creases flat, string wound and knotted.

2 — THE STORY

Before printed carry bags, every counter kept paper and string. Shopkeepers wrapped parcels in one practised sequence, hundreds of times a day.

3 — THE INTERPRETATION

A small choreography performed a hundred times daily — and noticed by no one until the day it is gone.

4 — THE QUESTION

How many small skills disappear unnoticed?

Sources: everyday market observation (generic setting); Gazetteer — bazaar commerce (general). Interpretive exhibit. Medium: original vector illustration.
NO. 05 — OLD BAZAAR
Artefact: a wooden sign, paint cracked by sun, lettering faded past reading. Fictitious lettering by design.

The Sign That Outlived Its Paint

Old bazaar street · wood and weather

1 — THE ARTEFACT

A hand-painted board, sun-cracked, its lettering worn past reading. Texture now, where a name used to be.

2 — THE STORY

Painted boards still hang over old-bazaar fronts, some older than the shops beneath them. Repainting is rarer than replacement — so the faded ones survive.

3 — THE INTERPRETATION

The street remembers in layers. Each faded board is a sentence the neighbourhood started and never finished.

4 — THE QUESTION

What disappears when an old sign disappears?

Sources: everyday street observation (generic setting); Gazetteer — old bazaars (general). No real sign depicted. Medium: original vector illustration.
NO. 06 — COUNTER DRAWER
Artefact: a cloth-bound ledger, soft pages, entries in fading ink. All entries fictional; nothing legible.

The Book That Remembers Who Owes What

Neighbourhood counter · credit by hand

1 — THE ARTEFACT

A handwritten ledger of monthly accounts. Borrowing and trust, recorded in ink — entries shown here are fictional.

2 — THE STORY

Neighbourhood shops long ran monthly accounts by hand. Credit was personal: the book recorded it, the relationship guaranteed it.

3 — THE INTERPRETATION

The ledger is not about money. It is a diary of who trusted whom — a social record wearing accountant’s clothes.

4 — THE QUESTION

What does a ledger reveal about relationships?

Sources: everyday market observation (generic setting); Gazetteer — commerce (general). No personal data shown. Medium: original vector illustration.
NO. 07 — SWEET LANE
Artefact: conical kulfi moulds, dented rims — and the kulfi that slides free, glistening.

The Mould That Gives a Memory Its Shape

Sweet lane · set and unmoulded daily

1 — THE ARTEFACT

Metal kulfi moulds filled with creamy mixture; the finished kulfi slid free, holding the mould’s exact form.

2 — THE STORY

Kulfi set in individual metal moulds is sold across old-city sweet lanes. The mould’s shape has barely changed across generations of vendors.

3 — THE INTERPRETATION

Taste forgets; shape remembers. The mould hands the same form to each generation and lets flavour do the rest.

4 — THE QUESTION

If the form remains, is the tradition still the same?

Sources: everyday market observation (generic setting); Gazetteer — food trade (general). Interpretive exhibit. Medium: original vector illustration.
NO. 08 — TEA STALL
Artefact: a steel tea glass — amber pour, rising steam, passed from stall to customer and back.

The Glass That Brings People Back

Roadside tea stall · washed and reused all day

1 — THE ARTEFACT

A small steel glass of tea, poured hot and passed across. Emptied, washed, and filled again within minutes.

2 — THE STORY

Roadside stalls serve tea in steel glasses reused all day. Regulars return to the same stall, the same glass, the same pause.

3 — THE INTERPRETATION

The glass belongs to no one and is missed by everyone. Belonging, it turns out, can be rented by the cup.

4 — THE QUESTION

Can an ordinary object represent belonging?

Sources: everyday market observation (generic setting); Gazetteer — street life (general). Interpretive exhibit. Medium: original vector illustration.
